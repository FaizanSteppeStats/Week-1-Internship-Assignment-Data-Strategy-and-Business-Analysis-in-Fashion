I am submitting my Week 3 assignment, “Exploratory Data Analysis and Visualization in Fashion Trends,” with a comprehensive Word report and a publicly accessible reproducible GitHub project. The assignment uses real, publicly published monthly U.S. clothing-store retail sales from the U.S. Census Bureau, redistributed through the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED). The source snapshot is dated October 15, 2026, and contains 92 consecutive monthly observations from January 2019 through August 2026. The report clearly states the geographic scope, monetary units and limitations of using national sales as a fashion-industry trend proxy.

My analysis begins with data acquisition, provenance documentation, type validation, chronological sorting, missing-value checks and monthly continuity checks. I then calculate descriptive statistics, annual summaries, year-over-year percentage changes, rolling averages and five-year monthly seasonality medians. The results reveal that median December clothing-store sales across 2021–2025 were 1.90 times the corresponding February median, indicating a strong holiday-season pattern. A median-absolute-deviation method identifies unusually large year-over-year shifts, including the genuine business disruption surrounding the 2020 pandemic, without incorrectly deleting real observations.

Five original Python-generated visualizations support the investigation: a time-series trend line with a 12-month rolling mean, a monthly seasonality bar chart, a year-wise boxplot, a related-series scatterplot and a clearly defined yearly-share pie chart. Each figure is interpreted in the report, with its appropriate use, potential misunderstanding and operational relevance explained. The report also evaluates correlation between clothing stores and a broader clothing-and-accessory series, explaining why related series can be highly correlated without proving independent causation.

Finally, I translate the observed patterns into practical merchandising, procurement and resource-planning hypotheses while distinguishing them from validated decisions for a particular retailer. I discuss data revisions, nominal versus real values, COVID-era outliers, seasonal adjustment, regional transferability and visualization risks, each with a targeted mitigation. The GitHub project includes source CSV, Python scripts, charts, numerical outputs, README, evaluator instructions and automated tests. All results can be independently reproduced without proprietary data.
